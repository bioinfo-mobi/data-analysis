Idea. Draw many samples of size n from a population, compute the mean of each, and look at the histogram of those means (the sampling distribution of x̄). The CLT says: it approaches N(μ, σ/√n), whatever the population.
  • Pick the exponential or lognormal population and increase n from 1 to 5, 30, 200: when does the bell shape appear?
  • Watch the observed SD of x̄ against σ/√n (the standard error) and the skewness shrinking like 1/√n.
  • Try the binary population (a "yes/no" variable, e.g. mutated vs. wild-type): x̄ is then a proportion.

Idea. A 95% CI is a procedure: if we repeat the experiment many times, ~95% of the intervals contain the true μ. Each horizontal line below is one experiment.
  • Increase the confidence level: intervals get wider, more of them cover μ. Decrease n: intervals get wider as well.
  • Choose "z with s plugged in" at n = 3–5: coverage falls below the nominal level – this is why we need the t-distribution.
  • Choose the lognormal population with small n: even the t-interval under-covers. Increase n and watch it recover (CLT).

Idea. For one observed sample (n, x̄, s), the CI uses the quantile t1−α/2, n−1 of the t-distribution. A test of H₀: μ = μ₀ uses the same ingredients – the two always agree.
  • Slide the confidence level: the red tail areas (total α) and the quantile move.
  • Slide n down to 2–3 and compare the t-distribution (blue) with the normal (grey dashed): heavy tails ⇒ larger quantile ⇒ wider CI.
  • Move μ₀ in and out of the CI: the p-value crosses α exactly when μ₀ leaves the interval.

Idea. The half-width of a CI is t·s/√n. Precision improves only with √n: to halve the interval you need 4× the samples.
  • Set the target margin and see how many samples you need – useful when planning an experiment.
  • Compare the curves for 90 / 95 / 99%: higher confidence costs samples.
  • Double s (more noise) and see how the required n grows.
